# Daylight Saving Time: A Brief, Bizarre History

Every March and November, hundreds of millions of people around the world change their clocks by one hour. The ritual is so universal that we rarely ask how it started — or why it persists despite a century of evidence that it's not actually doing what it was designed to do.

The history of Daylight Saving Time (DST) is a story of satire, war, marketing, and one of the strangest political footballs of the modern era.

## The 1784 Joke That Started It All

In April 1784, **Benjamin Franklin** — then living in Paris as the American ambassador — published a satirical letter in the *Journal de Paris* titled "An Economical Project for Diminishing the Cost of Candles."

Franklin's "project" was a tongue-in-cheek suggestion that Parisians could save millions of livres on candle wax by waking up earlier to take advantage of morning daylight. He noted, with mock astonishment, that most Parisians slept past dawn and burned candles for the first hour of the day, when the sun was already providing free light.

The letter was a joke. It made fun of Parisian laziness, not a serious proposal to change the clock. But it planted an idea that would resurface 130 years later.

## The First Real Proposal: 1895

The first serious proposal for DST came from **George Vernon Hudson**, a New Zealand entomologist who wanted more evening daylight to study insects after his day job. He presented the idea to the Wellington Philosophical Society in 1895, but it went nowhere.

In 1905, **William Willett**, a British builder and outdoorsman, independently hit on the same idea while riding his horse one summer morning and noticing how many blinds were still drawn at 6 AM. Willett spent the rest of his life campaigning for DST, writing a pamphlet called "The Waste of Daylight," and lobbying Parliament.

He died in 1915, a year before Britain finally adopted DST. He never got to see his idea implemented.

## World War I: The First Real Adoption

DST's first major adoption came in 1916, when **Imperial Germany** put the clocks forward on May 1 to save coal for the war effort. Britain, Russia, and most of Europe followed within weeks. The United States adopted DST in 1918 — also as a wartime measure — but repealed it in 1919 when the war ended.

For the next two decades, DST was a patchwork. Some US cities observed it, others didn't. Train schedules were chaos. The **Standard Time Act of 1918** had given the US its time zones, but DST remained a free-for-all until Congress passed the **Uniform Time Act of 1966**, which standardized the start and end dates nationwide.

## The 1973 Energy Crisis: DST Comes Back

DST was a minor curiosity until the **1973 oil embargo** quadrupled gasoline prices. Suddenly, the idea of saving energy by shifting daylight became politically urgent. Congress passed the **Emergency Daylight Saving Time Energy Conservation Act** in 1973, putting the entire US on permanent DST for two years.

The experiment was a bust. Energy savings turned out to be minimal — perhaps 1% of total electricity use — while the dark morning commutes caused a spike in traffic accidents and pedestrian deaths. The year-round DST experiment ended in 1975.

## The Modern Debate

Today, most of the debate around DST isn't about energy at all — it's about **health and safety**. A growing body of research shows that the spring-forward transition is associated with:

- A **6% increase** in fatal car accidents in the week after the change
- A **5% spike** in heart attacks on the Monday after spring-forward
- A measurable increase in workplace injuries and medical errors
- Disrupted sleep that takes **up to a week** to recover from

The **American Medical Association**, the **American Academy of Sleep Medicine**, and the **European Biological Rhythms Society** have all called for an end to the twice-yearly clock change. The question is what to replace it with.

## Permanent DST vs Permanent Standard Time

There are two options:

1. **Permanent DST** (clocks stay one hour ahead year-round). More evening daylight for shopping and recreation. But it means darker morning commutes in winter, especially for kids walking to school.

2. **Permanent Standard Time** (clocks stop changing). More morning daylight. But it means earlier sunsets in summer, which businesses and the recreation industry oppose.

The US **Sunshine Protection Act**, which would lock the country on permanent DST, passed the Senate unanimously in 2022. It has stalled in the House. Several states (California, Florida, Washington, Oregon) have already passed laws switching to permanent DST, but those laws require federal approval before they can take effect.

Other countries are in similar limbo. The **European Union voted in 2019** to end clock changes, but the directive has never been implemented. **Mexico abolished DST in 2022** for most of the country (except border cities that sync with the US).

## The Strange Persistence of a Wartime Measure

The one thing almost everyone agrees on is that the twice-yearly change is bad. Yet it persists, because the choice between permanent DST and permanent standard time creates winners and losers in roughly equal measure. Sunrise lobbies and sunset lobbies cancel each other out.

What began as a 1784 French joke, briefly became a WWI energy measure, then was tried again during the 1973 oil crisis, has now become one of the great political stalemates of the 21st century — a perfect example of how a temporary wartime policy can take on a life of its own.
