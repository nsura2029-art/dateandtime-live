# How Russia Decided to Span 11 Time Zones — and Then Reduced Them

Russia is the **most time-zone-spanning country on Earth**. At its longest, the country stretches across **11 distinct time zones** — from UTC+2 in Kaliningrad (the westernmost exclave) to UTC+12 in Kamchatka and Chukotka (the easternmost peninsula).

In 2010, then-President **Dmitry Medvedev** reduced the number of zones from 11 to 9, and in 2014, President **Vladimir Putin** ordered another reduction to 11 zones (the same number, but with different boundaries). The history of Russian time zones reads like a political diary: each change reflects the priorities of whoever was in power.

## Why So Many Zones?

Russia is **9,000 km wide** at its broadest. The sun rises in Kaliningrad 11 hours before it rises in Kamchatka. In the days before time zones, each town kept its own local solar time, which worked fine when most people never traveled more than 50 km from home.

When **standard time zones** were adopted in the 19th century, the Russian Empire drew zones based on the 1884 International Meridian Conference. After the 1917 revolution, the Soviet government reorganized time zones in 1919 — partly for efficiency, partly to symbolize the new state's reach.

By 1930, the USSR had standardized on **"Decree Time"** — every zone was set **one hour ahead** of its geographic solar time. The official reason was "to extend the working day" and save energy. In practice, it meant that in Moscow, the sun wouldn't reach its highest point until 1:30 PM during winter.

In 1981, the USSR adopted **summer time** (DST) for the entire country. From 1981 to 2011, Russia changed its clocks **every year** — sometimes twice. After the fall of the Soviet Union, the new Russian Federation kept the practice.

## The 2010–2014 Reforms

In **2011**, President Medvedev signed a law that:

- Reduced the number of time zones from **11 to 9**
- Made **summer time year-round** (so Russia stayed on "permanent DST" rather than switching back in autumn)
- Moved some regions 1 hour closer to Moscow, for political and economic alignment

The reform was widely unpopular. People complained that winter mornings were too dark, especially in the Far East. In 2014, Putin reversed the decision — and in doing so, he actually **increased the number of zones back to 11** (with some boundaries different from before).

The 2014 reform kept Russia on **permanent standard time** (no DST), and gave each region a zone aligned more closely with its geographic solar time. The official goal was to "reduce the negative health effects of the twice-yearly clock change."

## The Zones Today

As of 2026, Russia's 11 time zones are:

| Moscow | UTC+3 | Moscow, St. Petersburg, Sochi |
| Samara | UTC+4 | Samara, Udmurtia |
| Yekaterinburg | UTC+5 | Yekaterinburg, Tyumen, Orenburg |
| Omsk | UTC+6 | Omsk |
| Krasnoyarsk | UTC+7 | Krasnoyarsk, Norilsk |

The Moscow zone is by far the most populous, covering about **75 million people** — roughly half of Russia's population.

## The Curious Case of Yakutsk and Magadan

Two of the 11 zones are particularly unusual:

**Yakutsk (UTC+9)** is the coldest city on Earth, with winter temperatures regularly reaching **−40°C**. In 2014, when Russia moved from permanent DST back to permanent standard time, Yakutsk moved 1 hour earlier — meaning winter sunrises got later. Public protests followed, and the regional government lobbied (unsuccessfully) for a return to a more easterly zone.

**Magadan (UTC+11)** is so isolated that the local government briefly considered leaving Russia's time zone system entirely and adopting Tokyo time (UTC+9, then 1 hour behind Moscow). The proposal went nowhere, but it highlighted the political tension between regional identity and federal uniformity.

## Why Russia Keeps Changing

Three forces drive the constant changes:

1. **Geography** — Russia is so wide that any choice of zones is a compromise. Some regions will feel "wrong" no matter what.
2. **Politics** — zones are signals of alignment. Moving a region closer to Moscow time means moving it closer to the political center.
3. **Health** — research on DST and circadian rhythms has shifted public opinion. Russia went from "permanent DST" (2011) to "permanent standard" (2014) within three years.

The next change may come from **Sakhalin**, which has proposed switching from UTC+11 to UTC+10 to align with neighboring Khabarovsk. Or it may come from a complete abandonment of DST, which some regions have requested.

## What It Means for You

If you do business with Russia:

- **Check the zone for each specific city**, not the country. Moscow and Vladivostok are 7 hours apart, not "1 country" apart.
- **Russia is on permanent standard time** — no DST since 2014. So you don't need to track spring/fall changes.
- **Yakutsk has the largest gap** from Moscow (6 hours). Plan calls for working hours that are reasonable for both.
- **Kaliningrad is technically a different country** for time purposes — it's the only Russian zone at UTC+2.

Russia's time zones are a reminder that **time is political**. As long as Russia is the largest country on Earth, its time zone choices will shape global calendar debates.
