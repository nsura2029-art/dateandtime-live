# Why Some Countries Are 30 or 45 Minutes Off — The Strange World of Half-Hour Time Zones

Time zones were supposed to be simple. The Earth is 360 degrees, divided into 24 hours, so each zone covers 15 degrees and ticks over by exactly one hour. That was the theory, anyway.

In practice, **at least 12 countries** decided they preferred something a little weirder — and adopted offsets that fall between whole hours. India is 5h30 ahead of UTC. Iran is 3h30. Newfoundland is 3h30 *behind*. And two places on Earth — Nepal and Australia's Eucla region — use a 45-minute offset.

## Why 30 Minutes?

The history of half-hour zones is mostly about **national identity and political choice**, not geography.

**India's** UTC+5:30 was adopted in 1906 during British colonial rule. When the country gained independence in 1947, the government kept the offset — partly because changing it would mean recalibrating millions of clocks, and partly because **5:30 puts the sun's apex around 1:30 PM Indian Standard Time**, which the government argued was "good for productivity."

**Iran's** UTC+3:30 dates to 1910, when the country was struggling with multiple competing time standards. The 30-minute offset was a compromise. In 2022, Iran briefly abolished DST and adopted permanent UTC+3:30 — then re-introduced DST in 2023 after public complaints about dark winter mornings.

**Myanmar** is 30 minutes ahead of its neighbors (Bangladesh, India, Thailand). The reason? When Burma gained independence from Britain in 1948, the new government wanted to distinguish itself from India, which was at UTC+5:30. Adding 30 minutes put Burma at UTC+6:30, a different "slot" entirely.

## The 45-Minute Outliers

**Nepal** is UTC+5:45 — the only country in the world with a 45-minute offset. The story: in 1986, Nepal's government decided to distinguish itself from India's UTC+5:30. Rather than switch to UTC+6 (which would be an hour ahead of India), they chose the middle ground of UTC+5:45. The result is that Nepal's clocks are 15 minutes ahead of India and 1h15 ahead of Bangladesh.

**Chatham Islands** (New Zealand) is UTC+12:45. The remote Pacific territory uses a 45-minute offset so it can be a distinct time from mainland New Zealand while still being "in between" zones.

**Eucla** (Western Australia) is an unofficial time zone used by a small community near the WA-SA border. The Eucla Telegraph Station used a 45-minute offset to bridge Western and South Australian time. The community is tiny (about 50 people), but the offset persists informally.

## What It Means for You

Half-hour zones are a real headache for **international meeting planning**. A 9 AM call in London lands at 2:30 PM in India, 1:00 PM in Iran, and 4:45 PM in Nepal — none of which are at a clean "half hour" of the day.

If you work across these zones, the most reliable approach is:
- **Use a meeting planner that handles 15-minute increments** (not just 30-minute slots)
- **Always double-check the time zone abbreviation** (IST vs GMT+5:30, IRST vs IRDT, NPT vs GMT+5:45)
- **Set your phone to show the offset, not the abbreviation**, when traveling

The world is messier than 24 neat slices. But that's part of what makes time zones interesting.
