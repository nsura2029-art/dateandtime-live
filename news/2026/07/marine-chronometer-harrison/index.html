The Marine Chronometer and the Longitude Prize

📅 Published July 27, 2026 ⏱ 4 min read

In 1714, the British government offered £20,000 to anyone who could determine longitude at sea. John Harrison spent 40 years trying to claim it.

The Marine Chronometer and the Longitude Prize

For two centuries after the pendulum clock, the greatest unsolved problem in navigation was determining longitude at sea. A captain could easily measure latitude by the angle of the sun at noon, but longitude required knowing the exact time at a reference location — and no clock could keep accurate time on a rolling, storm-tossed ship.

The British government estimated that more sailors were dying from longitude errors than from war, shipwreck, and disease combined. In 1714, Parliament passed the Longitude Act, offering a prize of £20,000 (equivalent to about £12 million today) for a method that could determine longitude within half a degree.

The prize went unclaimed for decades. Then a self-taught English carpenter named John Harrison set out to win it.

The Longitude Problem

Longitude is a time problem. The Earth rotates 360° in 24 hours, so each degree of longitude equals 4 minutes of time. If you know the time at a fixed reference point (say, Greenwich) and the time at your current location (measured by the sun's position), the difference tells you how far east or west you are.

For example, if your local noon is 2 hours later than Greenwich noon, you're 30° west of Greenwich — somewhere in the mid-Atlantic.

The math was easy. The hardware wasn't. A useful marine clock had to:

  • Keep time to within 3 seconds per day (1 minute of longitude = 4 minutes of time = 1/2° = ~30 nautical miles)
  • Survive months at sea in rolling, pitching, temperature-varying, salt-spray conditions
  • Run without winding for months at a time
  • Be portable enough to carry on a small ship

No clock in 1714 could do all of this. The best pendulum clocks were accurate enough but couldn't survive ship motion. Pocket watches existed but were wildly inaccurate.

Harrison's 40-Year Quest

Harrison (1693–1776) was a Yorkshire carpenter with no formal education in clockmaking — but an obsessive mechanical intuition. In 1728, he began building his first sea clock, H1, completed in 1735. H1 was enormous — 66 pounds of brass, iron, and steel — but it was a marvel of anti-vibration design. On a trial voyage to Lisbon in 1736, it kept time to within 1 second per day of land-based reference clocks.

But the Board of Longitude — the committee that judged the prize — wanted more proof. Harrison spent the next 25 years building three more chronometers, each smaller and more accurate than the last:

  • H2 (1741) — refined design, but Harrison abandoned it to start over
  • H3 (1757) — incorporated a bimetallic strip to compensate for temperature, an invention still used in modern thermostats
  • H4 (1759) — a complete redesign. H4 looked like a large pocket watch (5 inches wide, 3 pounds) and kept time to within 1/3 second per day — three times better than the prize threshold

The Sea Trial

In 1761, Harrison's son William sailed H4 from Portsmouth to Kingston, Jamaica — a voyage of 81 days. When they arrived, H4 was found to be only 5 seconds slow compared to the land-based reference clock.

That translates to about 1.25 minutes of longitude, or roughly 1 mile at Jamaica's latitude — well within the prize threshold.

But the Board of Longitude refused to pay. They demanded a second trial, then a third. Harrison, by then in his late 60s, was bitter and exhausted. King George III personally tested H5 (a follow-up) at Kew Observatory in 1772, and the watch was found to be accurate to within 1/3 second per day — same as H4.

Parliament finally awarded Harrison the prize money in 1773, when he was 80 years old. He died three years later.

The Longitude Line

In 1884, an international conference chose Greenwich as the prime meridian — the line of 0° longitude. The reason was simple: the Royal Observatory at Greenwich had the best catalog of star positions and the best time-keeping (thanks to Harrison's chronometers, which had become standard equipment on British ships).

The Greenwich meridian gave rise to Greenwich Mean Time (GMT), the world's first standard time, and eventually to Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). Every time zone in the world is now defined as an offset from UTC, which is itself computed from atomic clocks but historically defined by the Greenwich meridian.

When you look at the time on your phone, you are looking at a number that descends in a direct line from Harrison's wooden workshop in Barrow upon Humber.

The Harrison Chronometers Today

H1, H2, H3, H4, and H5 are all preserved at the Royal Observatory, Greenwich, which is now a museum. H4 is occasionally taken out and demonstrated — and still works. The collection is considered one of the most important groups of historical scientific instruments in the world.

If you visit London, you can stand on the prime meridian line in Greenwich and see all five Harrison chronometers. The brass circles, the bimetallic strips, the ingenious anti-vibration mechanisms — they're all there, in a quiet room overlooking the Thames.
