The Quartz Revolution: How Seiko's 1969 Astron Rewrote Time

📅 Published July 27, 2026 ⏱ 5 min read

In 1969, a Japanese watchmaker released the first quartz wristwatch. Within a decade, the Swiss watch industry — centuries old — was in ruins.

The Quartz Revolution

On Christmas Day 1969, Seiko released the Astron — the world's first quartz wristwatch. It cost as much as a small car (¥450,000, or about $1,250 in 1969 dollars), and it kept time to within 5 seconds per month — about 100 times more accurate than any mechanical watch ever made.

Within a decade, the Swiss watch industry — which had dominated precision timekeeping since the 1600s — was in ruins. Tens of thousands of Swiss watchmakers lost their jobs. The Quartz Revolution is the most important event in the history of watchmaking, and it all comes down to a tiny piece of crystal.

The Magic of Quartz

Quartz is piezoelectric: when you apply a voltage to a quartz crystal, it vibrates. Conversely, when you bend a quartz crystal, it generates a voltage. The vibration frequency is extraordinarily stable — it depends mostly on how the crystal was cut, not on temperature or battery voltage.

A watch-sized quartz crystal, cut in a small tuning-fork shape, vibrates at exactly 32,768 Hz (2^15, a power of two because it makes the digital division circuits simple). The watch's circuit counts those 32,768 vibrations and divides by 2 repeatedly until it gets one pulse per second, which drives the second hand.

The result: a watch that gains or loses less than a second per day, and runs for a year on a single battery.

The Watch Industry Before Quartz

For 400 years, watchmaking was a mechanical craft. A good watch was a marvel of miniaturized engineering: dozens of gears, springs, jewels, and balance wheels, all hand-assembled in a clean room. A skilled watchmaker might spend weeks on a single pocket watch.

The industry was dominated by Switzerland — the words "Swiss made" were synonymous with precision. The country had a near-monopoly on high-end timekeeping, especially after John Harrison's chronometers made the British government a major buyer of Swiss watches in the 18th century.

By 1960, Swiss watchmakers employed about 90,000 people and exported $200 million worth of watches per year (about $2 billion in today's money). They had every reason to believe the future looked like the past.

Seiko vs Switzerland

The Quartz Revolution didn't come from Switzerland — it came from Seiko, a Japanese company founded in 1881. Seiko had been working on quartz timekeeping since the 1950s, first for the Japanese National Broadcasting Corporation (NHK) and then for consumer products.

The Astron, released in 1969, was the result of 20 years of patient research and billions of yen of investment. It was followed within 18 months by:

  • Güdel (Switzerland) — a competing quartz watch, but too late
  • Omega (Switzerland) — released the Marine Chronometer Quartz in 1970
  • Bulova, Seiko, Citizen — all released quartz models by 1972

Within five years, quartz watches were mass-produced in Hong Kong, Korea, and Taiwan at a fraction of the Swiss cost. By 1980, Swiss watch industry employment had fallen from 90,000 to 28,000. Exports collapsed.

The Swiss Comeback

The Swiss didn't give up. In 1983, the Swiss watch industry consolidated into a single conglomerate — SMH (later Swatch Group) — under the leadership of Nicolas Hayek. Instead of fighting the quartz revolution, they embraced it: the Swatch, a thin plastic quartz watch, became a fashion item and saved the industry by making watches fun and disposable.

Today, the Swiss watch industry is back to about 60,000 employees and exports ~$25 billion worth of watches per year. Most of those watches are quartz (the affordable end) or mechanical (the luxury end, where connoisseurs pay $50,000 for hand-assembled timepieces that are less accurate than a $20 Casio).

The Quartz Revolution didn't kill the Swiss watch industry — it bifurcated it. Quartz became the everyday default, mechanical became the luxury art form.

Silicon Valley's Debt to Quartz

The Quartz Revolution's impact goes far beyond watchmaking. The same quartz oscillators that made accurate wristwatches possible became the timing heart of every digital device.

  • Every computer has a quartz crystal that sets the clock speed
  • Every phone has a quartz oscillator (in addition to the atomic-clock-derived time)
  • Every radio, TV, and Wi-Fi router uses quartz to synchronize its transmissions
  • GPS satellites use quartz oscillators (calibrated to atomic clocks) to coordinate their signals

Without the Quartz Revolution, there would be no digital age as we know it. The first quartz watches in the 1970s led directly to the consumer electronics revolution of the 1980s and 1990s.

In Cupertino, California — the home of Apple — the descendants of those early quartz timekeepers are the timing crystals in every iPhone, iPad, and Mac. The history of Silicon Valley is, in a real sense, a continuation of the Quartz Revolution that began in a Tokyo watch factory in 1969.

What Quartz Can't Do

Quartz is amazing, but it's not perfect. Quartz oscillators drift with temperature — about 1 part per million per degree Celsius for a typical tuning-fork crystal. For most applications (watches, computers, phones), this is more than good enough. But for scientific precision, nothing beats an atomic clock.

Modern atomic clocks (see our article on NIST) are accurate to 1 part in 10^18 — 100 trillion times better than quartz. The future of precision timekeeping is atomic, not quartz. But for keeping your morning train on time, the humble quartz crystal still does the job.
